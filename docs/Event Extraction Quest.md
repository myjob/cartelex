## Claude:
this is a well-trodden problem space — what you're running into is essentially the standard pain of "naive event extraction" and there are mature patterns from temporal IE (information extraction) and the legal-tech world. Let me hit your three issues with the patterns I'd reach for.

**The meta-pattern: separate extraction from normalization from selection.** Most pipelines that struggle do everything in one pass. The robust setup is three stages: (1) extract raw event mentions liberally, (2) normalize and validate them against document-level context, (3) score and select for the final timeline. Your current pipeline collapses 2 and 3 into the loading step, which is why both noise and bad dates leak through.

**For (b), the broken dates — anchor-and-propagate.** SpaCy's NER and `dateparser` will happily produce "1001-01-19" because they parse digits without context. The standard fix is _temporal anchoring_:

- Before chunking, extract document-level anchors: the decision date, the case number/ECLI, the cited infringement period (cartel cases almost always state something like "from X to Y"). These come from the header and the operative part.
- When you encounter a partial date in a chunk (`23-Apr`, `April`, `Tuesday`), resolve it against the nearest preceding full date in the same paragraph, then the section anchor, then the document anchor — in that order. This is called _temporal context propagation_ and is what HeidelTime does well (better than spaCy for this — it was literally built for TempEval). Facebook's Duckling and Stanford's SUTime are similar.
- Add a hard validity gate: any normalized date outside `[document_date - 30 years, document_date + 5 years]` is flagged or dropped. Cartel cases rarely span more than a couple of decades; year 3790 is just impossible. This single check kills most garbage.
- For dates that genuinely can't be resolved, store them with an explicit `precision` field (`year`, `month`, `day`, `unknown`) instead of fabricating a full ISO date. Downstream code then knows not to plot them as point events.

**For (a), thousands of events on long cases — dedupe, classify, then summarize hierarchically.** A single cartel meeting gets mentioned in the Procedure section, the Facts, the Legal Assessment, and footnotes. Naive extraction multiplies this 4–10×. The patterns:

- _Event canonicalization_: cluster mentions by (normalized_date, participants, event_type) using your embeddings. Two events within the same day involving overlapping entities are almost always the same event. This alone usually cuts volume 60–80%.
- _Event typing_: classify each event into a small ontology (cartel meeting, communication, dawn raid, statement of objections, hearing, decision, appeal, judgment). You can do this with a small classifier or just an LLM call on the description. Then your timeline can be filtered by type — users rarely want all event types at once.
- _Hierarchical summarization_: keep two layers — a "headline timeline" (10–30 macro events: cartel formed, infringement period, raid, SO, decision, appeal, judgment) and a drill-down detail layer. The headline layer is generated by selecting events whose type is in a "milestone" set or whose salience score exceeds a threshold.

**For (c), critical dates hidden in verbosity — exploit document structure.** EU Commission decisions and CJEU judgments are highly structured. Don't treat them as flat text:

- Section-aware weighting: events in the _operative part_, _Procedure_ section, and recital headings ("Recital (X)") are far more likely to be canonical than events buried in the descriptive narrative. Tag chunks with their section before extraction and use section as a salience feature.
- Cue-phrase boosting: "the Commission adopted", "the infringement began", "by decision of", "pursuant to Article 101" — these are reliable signals that a critical date is nearby. A small list of cartel-case cue phrases gives you a cheap salience model.
- Cross-reference with known fields: most cartel cases have publicly available structured metadata (case number, decision date, fine, parties) on `eur-lex` or the DG COMP case register. If you pull these as ground truth anchors, you can both validate your extraction and prioritize events that align with them.

**Practical stack I'd suggest layering on what you have:**

- Replace or supplement spaCy's date NER with HeidelTime or `dateparser` configured with a `RELATIVE_BASE` set to your document anchor.
- Add an LLM-based second pass on each chunk that returns a strict Pydantic/JSON schema with `date`, `precision`, `event_type`, `entities`, `section`, `salience`. The schema constraint kills the "thousands of mush events" problem because you can require, e.g., `salience >= 3` for inclusion. Use a cheap model (Haiku-class) since you're doing lots of calls.
- Store everything in your pgvector table but add a `is_canonical` boolean and `cluster_id` so you can query the deduplicated timeline without losing the raw mentions for traceability.

If you want concrete starting points, the academic keyword to search is "temporal information extraction" + "document-level normalization" — the TempEval shared tasks and the recent work on legal event extraction (e.g., the LEGAL-BERT lineage, and papers around the EUR-Lex corpus) cover most of this. On the legal-tech side, vendors like Casetext, Luminance, and the EU's own Cellar/EUR-Lex pipelines all use some flavor of the anchor-propagate-validate pattern.

Want me to sketch out the Pydantic schema and validation logic for the second-pass extraction? That's usually where this kind of pipeline gets the biggest immediate quality win.


### HeidelTime Python Wrapper
Finding a reliable Python wrapper for **HeidelTime** can be challenging because HeidelTime itself is written in Java and requires several external dependencies (like TreeTagger and Perl).

Based on current repository activity and integration in other research projects, the following are the most trustworthy options on GitHub:

### 1. `py_heideltime` (Recommended)

This is currently the most popular and widely maintained wrapper. It is maintained by research groups (LIAAD/INESC TEC) and is designed to simplify the installation process, which is often the biggest hurdle with HeidelTime.

- **GitHub:** [https://github.com/hmosousa/py_heideltime](https://github.com/hmosousa/py_heideltime) (often referenced as `JMendes1995/py_heideltime`)
    
- **Installation:** `pip install py-heideltime`
    
- **Pros:** * Easier setup: It attempts to handle some of the "heavy lifting" regarding the Java/Perl dependencies.
    
    - Returns results as clean Python dictionaries (JSON-like) rather than raw XML.
        
    - Used as a core component in other reputable NLP tools like _Time-Matters_.
        
- **Usage:**
    
    Python
    
    ```
    from py_heideltime import heideltime
    text = "August 31st - News today that they are beginning to evacuate."
    results = heideltime(text, language='English')
    ```
    

### 2. `python_heideltime`

A more direct, lightweight wrapper that interfaces with the HeidelTime standalone `.jar`.

- **GitHub:** [https://github.com/PhilipEHausner/python_heideltime](https://github.com/PhilipEHausner/python_heideltime)
    
- **Pros:**
    
    - Provides a simple class-based interface (`Heideltime()`).
        
    - Good for users who want to manage their own HeidelTime standalone installation and just need a Python bridge.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - Less frequent updates than `py-heideltime`.
        
    - Requires you to manually configure the paths to your Java executable and HeidelTime standalone directory.
        

### Key Considerations for All Wrappers

Regardless of the wrapper you choose, you must ensure your system has the following installed, or the wrappers will fail:

1. **Java Runtime Environment (JRE):** HeidelTime is Java-based.
    
2. **Perl:** Required for the internal processing of HeidelTime.
    
3. **TreeTagger (Optional but recommended):** Most HeidelTime configurations require TreeTagger for Part-of-Speech tagging to achieve high accuracy.
    

**Summary:** If you want the most "modern" experience with the least configuration pain, use **`py-heideltime`**. If you already have HeidelTime running on your machine and just need a way to call it from a script, **`python_heideltime`** is a solid, no-frills choice.

## GPT-5.5

Your current pipeline is a good start, but for EU cartel/legal cases you probably need to separate **raw event extraction** from **canonical timeline construction**. The key is: do not try to turn every detected date into a final timetable entry immediately. Instead, keep a noisy evidence layer, then normalize, validate, merge, rank and only then produce the useful timeline.

Below is a practical architecture that addresses your three problems.

---

## 1. Use two timeline layers

### Layer 1: Raw extracted events

This contains everything you extract from chunks.

Example:

```json

{

"case_id": "AT.39226",

"chunk_id": "p_187",

"source_text": "On 23 April, the Commission sent a request for information to Lupin...",

"date_text": "23 April",

"normalized_start": null,

"normalized_end": null,

"date_precision": "day_month",

"inferred_year": null,

"event_type": "procedural",

"description": "Commission sent a request for information to Lupin.",

"actors": ["Commission", "Lupin"],

"confidence": 0.71

}

```

### Layer 2: Canonical events

This is the cleaned, deduplicated, ranked timeline you actually use downstream.

Example:

```json

{

"case_id": "AT.39226",

"canonical_event_id": "evt_00091",

"start_date": "2009-04-23",

"end_date": null,

"date_precision": "day",

"event_type": "procedural",

"importance": "medium",

"description": "The Commission sent a request for information to Lupin.",

"supporting_raw_events": ["raw_1221", "raw_1244"],

"evidence_paragraphs": ["p_187", "p_190"]

}

```

This lets you preserve noisy evidence without letting it pollute your final timeline.

## 2. Do not store fake dates for incomplete dates

This is the source of problems like:

```text

1001-01-19: Internal presentation assessment of Lupin dossier

3790-...

```

If the source says only:

```text

23 April

```

do not coerce it into a full SQL date.

Store it as a partial temporal expression.

Recommended fields:

```sql

CREATE TABLE raw_events (

id bigserial PRIMARY KEY,

case_id text NOT NULL,

chunk_id text,

paragraph_id text,

date_text text,

year int,

month int,

day int,

date_precision text, -- 'year', 'month', 'day', 'day_month', 'range', 'unknown'

normalized_start date,

normalized_end date,

inferred boolean DEFAULT false,

inference_source text, -- 'same_paragraph', 'section_heading', 'document_date', etc.

date_confidence numeric,

event_type text,

description text,

actors text[],

source_text text,

embedding vector

);

```

For example:

| Source text | Store as |

|---|---|

| `23 April` | `day=23`, `month=4`, `year=null`, `date_precision='day_month'` |

| `April 2009` | `year=2009`, `month=4`, `day=null`, `date_precision='month'` |

| `in 2009` | `year=2009`, `date_precision='year'` |

| `between 2005 and 2008` | `normalized_start='2005-01-01'`, `normalized_end='2008-12-31'`, `date_precision='range_year'` |

| `on 23 April 2009` | full date |

Only create `normalized_start` when you are confident.

## 3. Add strict date validation

Before accepting a normalized date, validate it against legal/domain constraints.

For EU competition/cartel cases, a reasonable generic year range might be:

```text

1957 <= year <= current_year + 2

```

But even better, compute a case-specific range.

For example:

```text

earliest_possible_year = 1957

latest_possible_year = judgment_year + 1

```

If a case judgment is from 2014, then a parsed year like 3790 or 1001 should be rejected automatically.

Pseudo-code:

```python

def validate_year(year, case_context):

if year is None:

return True

min_year = 1957

max_year = case_context.get("judgment_year", datetime.now().year) + 2

return min_year <= year <= max_year

```

Also reject suspicious dates extracted from:

```text

Case T-379/10

Case C-100/19

Article 101

Regulation No 1/2003

paragraph 3790

```

These are frequent sources of false dates.

Add blacklist/context rules:

```python

BAD_DATE_CONTEXT_PATTERNS = [

r"Case\s+[CTF]-?\s*\d+/\d+",

r"Article\s+\d+",

r"Regulation\s+No\s+\d+/\d+",

r"paragraph\s+\d+",

r"recital\s+\d+",

r"\bT-\d+/\d+\b",

r"\bC-\d+/\d+\b"

]

```

If a date candidate appears inside one of those spans, discard it.

## 4. Use temporal anchoring instead of guessing

Many legal documents have local temporal context.

Example:

```text

2009

On 23 April, the Commission sent a request for information.

On 15 May, Lupin replied.

```

Here, `23 April` and `15 May` should inherit `2009`.

But only infer year if the anchor is reliable.

Useful anchoring hierarchy:

1. Same sentence: highest confidence

2. Same paragraph: high confidence

3. Nearby heading: medium confidence

4. Previous dated event in same section: medium/low confidence

5. Document date: low confidence

6. No anchor: do not infer

Example logic:

```python

def infer_year(event, context):

if event.year:

return event.year, "explicit", 1.0

if context.same_paragraph_year:

return context.same_paragraph_year, "same_paragraph", 0.9

if context.section_heading_year:

return context.section_heading_year, "section_heading", 0.75

if context.previous_event_year and context.same_section:

return context.previous_event_year, "previous_event_same_section", 0.6

return None, None, 0.0

```

Important: if you infer the year, store that it was inferred.

```json

{

"date_text": "23 April",

"normalized_start": "2009-04-23",

"inferred": true,

"inference_source": "section_heading",

"date_confidence": 0.75

}

```

## 5. Extract dates first, events second

Instead of extracting events from arbitrary 400-token chunks, use a date-centric approach.

Current approach:

```text

chunk -> extract events -> extract dates

```

Better approach:

```text

document -> detect date mentions -> create local date windows -> extract event around each date

```

For each detected date, take a local window:

```text

previous sentence + date sentence + next sentence

```

or:

```text

paragraph containing date

```

This solves your problem:

> critical dates are hidden in the verbosity

Because you focus the extraction on date-bearing passages.

Example:

```python

for date_mention in detected_dates:

window = get_surrounding_sentences(date_mention, before=1, after=1)

event = extract_event_from_window(window, date_mention)

```

This is often much more accurate than asking the model or SpaCy to summarize a whole long chunk.

## 6. Use section-aware chunking, not fixed 400-token chunking

Legal documents are structured. You should exploit that.

Prefer chunking by:

1. Headings

2. Numbered paragraphs

3. Recitals

4. Sentences

5. Then token length if necessary

EU cases often have sections like:

```text

Background to the dispute

Administrative procedure

The contested decision

The procedure before the General Court

Forms of order sought

Law

```

The date importance depends heavily on the section.

For example:

| Section | Timeline usefulness |

|---|---|

| Administrative procedure | High |

| Background to the dispute | High |

| The cartel/infringement | Very high |

| Law / Pleas in law | Medium/low |

| Arguments of the parties | Often noisy |

| Costs | Low |

So store section metadata:

```json

{

"section": "Administrative procedure",

"paragraph_number": 42,

"chunk_text": "..."

}

```

Then use it for ranking.

## 8. Deduplicate aggressively

Legal judgments repeat the same procedural dates many times.

You need event merging.

Merge if events have:

```text

same case_id

same normalized date or overlapping date range

same event_type

similar actors

similar embedding

similar description

```

Example:

```python

def should_merge(e1, e2):

if e1.case_id != e2.case_id:

return False

if e1.normalized_start != e2.normalized_start:

return False

if e1.event_type != e2.event_type:

return False

if cosine_similarity(e1.embedding, e2.embedding) < 0.86:

return False

return True

```

After merging:

```json

{

"date": "2009-04-23",

"description": "The Commission sent Lupin a request for information.",

"support_count": 5,

"source_paragraphs": [42, 87, 133, 201, 244]

}

```

This alone can reduce thousands of raw events to a few hundred or fewer.

## 9. Build hierarchical timelines

For long cartel cases, one flat timetable is often the wrong output.

Create several levels:

### Level 1: Executive timeline

10–30 events.

```text

1998–2004: Cartel conduct took place.

2005-10-20: Commission carried out inspections.

2008-07-08: Commission adopted the decision.

2010-06-15: General Court action lodged.

2014-09-09: General Court delivered judgment.

```

### Level 2: Procedural timeline

Only administrative and court procedure.

### Level 3: Infringement timeline

Only cartel/conduct facts.

### Level 4: Evidence-level raw timeline

All extracted events, including low-level correspondence.

This prevents the “thousands of events” problem. You do not need to delete raw events; you just should not expose them all as the main timeline.

## 10. Use salience scoring

Assign every canonical event an importance score.

Example scoring features:

```text

+ date is complete

+ event type is high-value

+ appears in important section

+ contains key legal terms

+ mentioned multiple times

+ involves Commission, General Court, Court of Justice, undertaking

+ event is near words like "decision", "infringement", "fine", "inspection", "appeal"

- date is partial

- event appears only in case-law citation section

- date is from unrelated precedent

- event is merely an internal document reference

```

Example:

```python

KEY_TERMS = {

"high": [

"decision", "infringement", "fine", "penalty", "inspection",

"dawn raid", "statement of objections", "oral hearing",

"leniency", "cartel", "price fixing", "market sharing",

],

"low": [

"internal presentation", "annex", "footnote", "case-law",

"costs", "plea", "argument"

]

}

```

Then only include events above a threshold in the main timeline.

## 12. Recommended pgvector setup

Use pgvector for semantic similarity, but do not rely on it for date filtering. Dates and event types should be normal indexed columns.

Example schema:

```sql

CREATE TABLE canonical_events (

id bigserial PRIMARY KEY,

case_id text NOT NULL,

start_date date,

end_date date,

date_precision text NOT NULL,

inferred boolean DEFAULT false,

date_confidence numeric,

event_type text,

importance_score numeric,

description text NOT NULL,

actors text[],

source_paragraphs int[],

raw_event_ids bigint[],

embedding vector(1536)

);

CREATE INDEX canonical_events_case_date_idx

ON canonical_events(case_id, start_date);

CREATE INDEX canonical_events_type_idx

ON canonical_events(event_type);

CREATE INDEX canonical_events_embedding_idx

ON canonical_events

USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops);

```

For partial dates, either keep them in separate fields or use nullable `start_date`.

```sql

year int,

month int,

day int

```

Do not force every event into a PostgreSQL `date`.

## Gemini

Extracting structured timelines from dense legal text is a classic NLP challenge. The pipeline you've built (Chunking -> SpaCy NER/Extraction -> Condensation -> pgvector) is a solid architectural foundation, but it’s running into the standard limitations of purely deterministic or localized extraction. Legal text is highly referential, and isolating chunks strips away the temporal context needed to make sense of incomplete dates.

Here is a breakdown of how to adapt your pipeline to solve these three specific challenges.

### A) Taming the Excessive Timelines (The Volume Problem)

Thousands of events per case usually mean your pipeline is extracting _every_ temporal occurrence rather than _salient legal events_.

- **Implement Event Typing:** Instead of extracting every verb associated with a date, define a strict ontology of event types (e.g., `Infringement_Start`, `Dawn_Raid`, `Statement_of_Objections`, `Fining_Decision`, `Appeal`). Map your extracted events to this ontology and discard the rest.
    
- **Cross-Chunk Deduplication:** Legal documents reiterate timelines constantly (e.g., "As established in the April 23 meeting..."). You need a clustering step before pushing to pgvector. You can use a lightweight embedding model to cluster your condensed descriptions; if two events have high cosine similarity and fall within the same 30-day window, merge them.
    
- **Hierarchical Summarization:** If a cartel lasted 15 years, you don't need every internal email. Group extracted events by quarter or year, and pass those batches through an LLM to generate a single "Period Summary" event.
    

### B) Fixing Temporal Hallucinations (The Year 1001 / 3790 Problem)

When SpaCy (or a downstream date parser like `dateutil` or `dateparser`) sees "23-Apr" without a year, it often defaults to the current year, 1900, or misinterprets surrounding numerical noise as a year.

- **Temporal Anchoring (Stateful Processing):** Stop processing chunks in isolation. Pass a `current_reference_year` variable along with your chunk iteration.
    
    - Extract the primary date of the document (usually at the top).
        
    - As you iterate chronologically through the text, if a chunk mentions a year (e.g., "In 2005..."), update the `current_reference_year`.
        
    - If you hit "23-Apr" and the parser fails to find a year, inject the `current_reference_year` before parsing.
        
- **HeidelTime integration:** Swap standard date parsers for **HeidelTime** (via `python-heideltime`). It is specifically designed for relative time extraction (e.g., "two days later", "in May") and uses document creation time to anchor partial dates far better than standard libraries.
    
- **Sanity Bounding Boxes:** Implement hard heuristic filters. The EU (and its predecessors) have specific establishment dates. Any date before ~1950 or after the current year is mathematically invalid. Write a fallback script that catches these anomalies and overwrites them with the `current_reference_year`.
    

### C) Finding Critical Dates Hidden in Verbosity (The Signal-to-Noise Problem)

Legal verbosity buries the lead. If you are extracting dates just because a date exists in a sentence, you will extract a lot of irrelevant procedural noise.

- **Dependency-Driven Extraction:** Don't start with the date; start with the action. Use SpaCy's dependency parser to look for critical lemmas (`fine`, `infringe`, `participate`, `appeal`, `adopt`). Once you find a critical verb, traverse the dependency tree to find the attached `DATE` or `TIME` entity. If a date isn't modifying a legally significant action, drop it.
    
- **Query-Driven Extraction (Reverse the Flow):** Since you are already using pgvector, you can flip your architecture. Instead of extracting all dates and putting them in the DB, put the _raw text chunks_ in pgvector.
    
    - Query the DB with specific vectors: "When did the commission conduct inspections?" or "When did the cartel agreement start?"
        
    - Retrieve the top 3-5 relevant chunks.
        
    - Run a strong extraction model (like a lightweight LLM) _only_ on those highly relevant chunks to pull the exact dates.

## DeepSeek

You’re hitting exactly the kind of edge cases that turn a “works mostly okayish” pipeline into a fragile mess. The core issues you’re seeing—event flooding, nonsensical default dates, and critical dates buried in noise—all have practical, targeted fixes without necessarily throwing out your spaCy-based approach.

Let’s take each challenge in turn.

---

### a) Excessive events in long-running cartels

Long procedural histories naturally produce thousands of date mentions. The problem is that naive extraction treats every date-bearing sentence as an event of equal weight. You need **event salience filtering** and **deduplication/merging**.

**Practical strategies:**

- **Define a taxonomy of key procedural milestones**  
  EU competition cases have a predictable backbone: dawn raid, Statement of Objections, oral hearing, Commission decision, appeal to General Court, appeal to ECJ, fine payment, leniency application, etc. Build a small list of 15–25 milestone types with trigger phrases. Then, during extraction, classify each spaCy-extracted event against this taxonomy (zero-shot with a small transformer model like `facebook/bart-large-mnli` or even simple keyword matching). Keep only events that match a milestone, or that have a classifier confidence > 0.7.

- **Cluster nearby events**  
  When the same legal step spawns multiple date mentions (e.g., “On 14 March 2003, the Commission carried out inspections… On the following day, 15 March, it continued…”), merge them into a composite event or pick the first mention as the anchor. You can group events whose extracted dates fall within a short window (say 3 days) and whose spaCy descriptions are cosine-similar above a threshold.

- **Use a hierarchical summary**  
  Instead of loading every extracted event into pgvector, first build a raw timeline, then run a second pass that selects the top-k events per year, or per procedural phase. You can score events by:
  - presence of legally significant verbs (adopt, annul, uphold, impose a fine)
  - number of named entities (authorities, company names)
  - proximity to section headings (“the decision”, “appeal”)
  This way, even for decade-long cartels, you keep a manageable 30–100 critical events.

---

### b) Missing date parts leading to weird years (1001, 3790)

SpaCy’s date parser (and many libraries) will default missing components to the current date or to epoch zero when forced to produce a datetime. Without a full date, you get pathological years like `1001` or `3790` if the regex-based filler goes wild.

**How to clean this up:**

- **Switch to a more controllable date parser**  
  Use `dateparser` with settings that prevent wild defaults:
  ```python
  import dateparser
  parsed = dateparser.parse("23-Apr", settings={
      'PREFER_DATES_FROM': 'past',
      'REQUIRE_PARTS': ['day', 'month'],  # won’t fill year
  })
  ```
  Then, if the year is missing, treat the date as incomplete and **do not assign a dummy year**. Store the date as a partial (e.g., `04123` as day-month) and later try to infer the year from the document’s metadata (decision date, case year range). A good rule of thumb: if the extracted year differs from the document’s date by more than 10 years and is not explicitly stated, discard it or flag it.

- **Post-validation heuristics**  
  Discard any extracted date with a year before 1900 or after 2050 (or whatever window fits EU cartel law). For any date that passes, also check that the month is 1–12 and day ≤ 31. This catches the `3790` nonsense.

- **Re-parse with context**  
  If the chunk says “23-Apr”, it usually refers to the year that is clear from the surrounding document. Maintain a running “document year” assumption (from the most recent full date) and fill missing years only when the parsed date is within a plausible offset (e.g., ±1 year) from that anchor. So if the document narrates events of 2003 and you hit “23-Apr”, set it to 23 April 2003.

---

### c) Critical dates hidden in verbose text

When you chunk naively at ~400 tokens, you might cut off the very sentence that gives a date its context, or you might extract a date but then generate a description based on a chunk that doesn’t contain the event’s legal significance.

**Fixes that preserve meaning:**

- **Extract dates in a full-document first pass, then attach context**  
  Rather than chunk→spaCy→date extraction, run a dedicated date extraction (regex + `dateparser`) across the entire document first. Collect a list of `(date_string, span_start, span_end)` positions. Then, for each date, extract a window of 2–3 surrounding sentences (not tokens) using a sentence segmenter. That window becomes the basis for your “meaningful description”. This avoids splitting a key date from its verb (e.g., “The Commission adopted the decision on [date]” stays whole).

- **Use layout cues**  
  EU judgments and decisions often have numbered paragraphs, footnotes, and section headings. Dates mentioned in the body of a section titled “Procedure” or “Background” are far more likely to be critical. You can weight events higher if they appear in those sections (simple regex on the preceding headings). Many documents even italicise or parenthetically mark the date of the operative part—these are gold.

- **Leverage a small fine-tuned NER model**  
  If spaCy’s generic NER is missing dates because they’re formatted unusually or hidden, consider fine-tuning a spaCy model on a few dozen manually annotated EU cartel cases. You can also swap the NER component for a transformer-based one (e.g., `en_legal_ner_trf` if available) that handles legal long-form dates much better.

- **Post-extraction “key sentence” scoring**  
  For each date window, score the sentence that contains the date using a short list of indicative verbs (fine, annul, appeal, inspect, infringe, leniency). Use that sentence—not the whole chunk summary—as the event description.

---

### Putting it all together (suggested pipeline)

1. **Pre-process** full text: identify sections (procedure, facts, law), anchor year from the document’s own date.
2. **Date spotting pass**: regex + `dateparser` across the entire raw text, yielding a list of (date, sentence_context). Keep only plausible dates (1900–2050).
3. **Event classification**: For each date, classify its surrounding sentence(s) into milestone types. Discard any not matching a milestone type.
4. **Timeline consolidation**: Merge events that are identical in type and closer than 3 days. Fill missing years from document year only when safe.
5. **Load into pgvector**: Now you have a clean, deduplicated list of critical events, each with a crisp one-sentence description.

This approach keeps the core spaCy work you already have but adds guardrails that directly address all three pain points. You’ll end up with a consistent timeline of legally meaningful events, no phantom years, and far fewer events—while still capturing everything that matters.

Short answer: there's no single library that does all three (interval + granularity + confidence) cleanly. You assemble it from two or three pieces. Here's the landscape.

**For interval + granularity**

_`python-dateutil`_ — parses partial ISO strings but returns points, not intervals. You'd add the interval logic yourself (~20 lines). Most common choice.

_`pendulum`_ — has a proper `Period` / `Interval` type and clean `add(months=2)` arithmetic. Better ergonomics than dateutil for the duration-anchoring step. Still doesn't model granularity natively; you carry that as a sidecar field.

_`whenever`_ (newer, ~2024) — strict typing, explicit about precision, but doesn't model partial dates as first-class either.

_`edtf-python`_ (Extended Date/Time Format, Library of Congress spec) — **this is the one most people miss and it's the closest fit for your problem.** EDTF was designed precisely for "uncertain, approximate, or partial" dates in archival/legal/historical contexts. It natively represents:

- `1997` (year precision), `1997-02` (month precision)
- `1997?` (uncertain), `1997~` (approximate), `1997%` (both)
- `[1996, 1997]` (one-of), `{1996, 1997}` (all-of)
- Open intervals: `1997/..`, `../1997`
- Seasons, decades, centuries with precision flags

The `edtf` Python package parses these and gives you `lower_strict()` / `upper_strict()` / `lower_fuzzy()` / `upper_fuzzy()` methods — which map directly onto your interval-with-granularity model. The fuzzy bounds even give you a natural place to encode "this is approximate" without inventing your own confidence scheme. For legal documents with phrases like "around March 1997" or "sometime in 1996", EDTF is genuinely the right vocabulary. It's a Library of Congress standard, so it's stable and auditable.

**For TimeML/TIMEX3-native handling**

_`timex3-utilities` and `python-timex`_ exist but are thin and unmaintained. The TimeML ecosystem largely stopped at the Java tools (HeidelTime itself, CAEVO, TARSQI). If you want the full TimeML data model in Python, you're mostly building it yourself or wrapping the Java tools.

_`sutime`_ (Stanford) via `py-sutime` — alternative to HeidelTime, returns intervals natively for some expressions. Won't help you post-process HeidelTime output, but worth knowing.

**For confidence**

Nothing gives you this out of the box. Confidence has to come from your pipeline:

- **Parse confidence** — did normalization succeed cleanly (high), require fallback heuristics (medium), or fail and get guessed (low)? Trivial to assign rule-based.
- **Granularity penalty** — year-level less confident than day-level for downstream reasoning. Also rule-based.
- **Anchoring confidence** (for durations) — heuristic match score, dependency-parse distance, or LLM-reported confidence. This is where you'd actually compute something.
- **Calibration** — if you want real probabilities, hand-label 100–200 examples and fit a small classifier on the features above. Overkill unless you're doing this at scale.

**What I'd actually wire together**

```
HeidelTime output
    │
    ▼
edtf-python  ──►  (lower_strict, upper_strict, granularity)
    │
    ▼
your code   ──►  confidence score from parse outcome + 
                  granularity + (for durations) anchor quality
    │
    ▼
pandas DataFrame with the schema from before
```

`edtf` for the hard part (representing partial/uncertain dates correctly), `pendulum` or `dateutil` for duration arithmetic, your own ~30 lines for the confidence rubric. That's the whole thing.

One caveat: HeidelTime emits TIMEX3 values, not EDTF. They overlap heavily for common cases (`1997`, `1997-02`, `1997-02-14`) but diverge for fuzzy expressions — HeidelTime uses `XXXX` placeholders and modifier attributes, EDTF uses `?` `~` `%`. You'll write a small adapter (HeidelTime → EDTF string) before feeding to the parser. Maybe 40 lines, mostly a lookup table for the modifier attributes.

# TimeML successors and modern temporal annotation

what has replaced the rise of TimeML such as sutime & heideltime?

The short answer is that **nothing has cleanly "replaced" them** — SUTime and HeidelTime are still around and still cited as baselines — but the field has moved in three directions, and most modern practitioners pick one of these instead.

**1. Transformer-based temporal taggers (the direct successor)**

Starting around 2021, neural taggers built on BERT/RoBERTa took over the research SOTA. The most-cited work is Almasian, Aumiller & Gertz, "BERT got a Date: Introducing Transformers to Temporal Tagging" (2021/2022), which interestingly uses HeidelTime to weakly label a large news corpus, then trains a RoBERTa encoder-decoder on top. A transformer encoder-decoder model using the RoBERTa language model was the best performing system and surpassed previous works in temporal tagging and type classification, especially on rare classes. The published HuggingFace checkpoints (`satyaalmasian/temporal_tagger_roberta2roberta`, `temporal_tagger_BERT_tokenclassifier`, etc.) are what most people reach for now if they want a drop-in neural replacement that still emits TIMEX3. There are also language-specific variants like Hengam for Persian and TEER for clinical English/Chinese.

**2. Lightweight rule/ML hybrids that are easier to deploy**

Because HeidelTime is a heavy Java/UIMA dependency and SUTime drags in all of Stanford CoreNLP, a lot of production work has migrated to lighter tools that don't strictly follow TimeML but cover the same use cases:

- **Duckling** (Facebook/Wit.ai) — probabilistic CFG, multilingual, very common in chatbots.
- **dateparser** (Python) — a popular library for parsing free-form date strings into datetimes.
- **spaCy + Timexy** — a spaCy extension that does TIMEX3-style annotation inline in the spaCy pipeline.
- **Stanza** — Stanford's PyTorch successor to CoreNLP, which still wraps SUTime but in a friendlier package.

**3. LLMs doing it zero-/few-shot**

The biggest practical shift is that for many applications people now just ask a general-purpose LLM (GPT-4-class or Claude) to extract and normalize temporal expressions directly, often with a JSON schema or structured-output constraint. This sidesteps the TimeML/TIMEX3 ecosystem entirely. A 2025 review of transformer-based temporal IE notes that small gains in state-of-the-art performance from one model to the next probably represent additional hyperparameter tuning more than substantial progress in understanding the relations between temporal entities in text — and that most evaluation still happens on a handful of small newswire datasets, which is part of why dedicated taggers haven't seen a clean generational replacement the way, say, NER did.
[[https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07470v1]]

**The other thing worth knowing**: TimeML/TIMEX3 itself has competition. SCATE (Semantically Compositional Annotation for Time Expressions, Bethard & Parker 2016) is the main alternative annotation scheme, and Chrono is the tagger associated with it — particularly in clinical NLP, where SCATE handles relative/anchored expressions ("two weeks before admission") more cleanly than TIMEX3.

So in practice: if you want a research-grade neural tagger, use the Almasian et al. RoBERTa models; if you want something lightweight and production-friendly, use Duckling or dateparser or spaCy+Timexy; if you're already calling an LLM, just ask it. HeidelTime and SUTime are still respectable baselines and still maintained, just no longer the default starting point.
