**What's genuinely in a dead end.** The heavyweight formal stack — hand-authored OWL-DL, description-logic reasoners, SPARQL-first everything, and especially the _waterfall_ where you model a large TBox before you've seen a single query — that ceremony is fading, and reasonably so. Two forces killed it. First, capable LLMs absorbed most of what lightweight ontologies used to buy you: type inference, relation naming, entity disambiguation, coreference. You no longer need to encode "a subsidiary is-a company" explicitly for the model to use it. Second, for a large class of mid-complexity apps, hybrid search over a sane relational schema in Postgres does 90% of what a dedicated graph database was sold to do, without the operational tax. So "RDF + big upfront ontology + Neo4j as a prerequisite" being overkill is a defensible read of 2025–2026.

**What's not dying — it got cheaper and rebranded.** The part that's actually resurgent is _explicit relational structure over extracted facts_, which is now marketed as GraphRAG. The thing that historically made knowledge graphs "not worth it" was construction and maintenance cost: weeks to months of ontology and extraction labor. LLMs collapse exactly that cost. Few-shot extraction with a strong model reaches accuracy roughly on par with — sometimes above — fully supervised traditional models, without thousands of labeled examples. The 2025 survey literature on LLM-empowered KG construction is largely about this: the model generates competency questions, drafts the schema, then does ontology-grounded triple extraction under that schema. So the barrier that made you skip structure is the barrier that fell — which cuts _toward_ keeping structure, not away from it.

**Where the industry actually landed: routing, not either/or.** When RAG and GraphRAG were run head-to-head across unified task sets in 2025, neither won outright — plain RAG took single-hop and detail-oriented questions, GraphRAG took multi-hop and global-sensemaking questions, and combining them at the response stage beat the best single baseline. The consensus framing from the Neo4j "GraphRAG Manifesto" through Microsoft's GraphRAG is not vector _or_ graph, but vector _plus_ graph, with a router sending each query to the backend that fits its type — semantic retrieval for high-volume similarity, graph traversal for relational reasoning and explainability. That's the whole game now.

**Why this matters specifically for celex-facts — and where your "skip it" instinct would bite you.** Here's the thing: you've already built the valuable 80% of a knowledge graph, you just didn't call it RDF. Your `fact_registry` + `fact_source_link` + recursive provenance traversal _is_ a property graph with typed edges living in Postgres. The question was never "should I bolt a KG on" — it's "which queries can I not serve without the structure I already have." For a legal/competition-law corpus, four buckets fall squarely on the structured side:

- _Multi-hop relational reasoning_ — cartel → participants → parent entity via LEI → other cartels that parent touched → decisions → appeals → courts. This is precisely the multi-hop bucket where vector retrieval fragments and graph structure wins. In the legal literature this is a named failure: LLM outputs are fluent but often shallow, mimicking surface patterns without reconstructing the logic of legal inference.
    
- _Exhaustive enumeration and aggregation_ — "cartel periods per year across the corpus," "all courts appearing in a document," "every cartel firm X participated in." Vector similarity structurally cannot answer these: it returns top-k, not the complete set. Your compositional analytical query layer (population resolution → projection → aggregation) exists because embeddings can't do this, and no amount of Qwen-32B horsepower changes that — it's a recall-completeness problem, not a reasoning-capacity problem.
    
- _Provenance and verifiability_ — the legal domain punishes misattribution, and structured facts act as a hard constraint: if the graph holds no relationship between two entities, retrieval won't supply it, which sharply reduces fabrication. Your atomic-proposition verification with decoupled entailment is this idea done well.
    
- _Temporal validity_ — this one you already got burned by, with the pre-Lisbon Article 81/82 EC vs. post-2009 101/102 TFEU numbering. Temporal blindness is a specifically cited weakness of chunk-based vector RAG; your HeidelTime/TIMEX3 temporal roles are the structured fix, and embeddings don't give you that for free.
    

**The Qwen-32B point, inverted.** The evidence argues against using a medium model _as a substitute_ for the graph on precise relational retrieval. On CypherBench, GPT-4o reaches only about 60% execution accuracy on precise retrieval over full-scale graphs, and no model under 10B parameters clears 20%. Medium models are excellent at two jobs — _building_ the graph (extraction) and _reading_ retrieved structured context to synthesize an answer — but they are not reliable at _being_ the relational index. So the right division of labor is: Qwen 32B constructs and synthesizes; the structured store retrieves precise multi-hop and aggregate facts; the router decides. That's basically what your DSPy `LegalQA` / `PlanRetrieval` / `GenerateKBSQL` split already encodes.

So my honest read: kill the RDF/OWL/SPARQL/dedicated-graph-DB ambition if you were still tempted by it — pgvector plus a typed relational fact model is a fully legitimate "graph," and Postgres is the right substrate for you. But don't generalize that into "structure isn't required." You'd be dropping exactly the capabilities (enumeration, aggregation, multi-hop provenance, temporal validity) that a legal fact system lives or dies on, and that hybrid search alone provably doesn't cover. The modern architecture _is_ the hybrid one you're already most of the way to.

Two caveats worth holding: LLM-built graphs inherit extraction noise and entity-resolution errors, so construction isn't free correctness — it moves the QA burden, it doesn't remove it. And if graph-construction token cost ever bites at your corpus size, LightRAG is the usual answer — roughly comparable accuracy to GraphRAG at about a 10x token reduction via dual-level retrieval.

Reading list, most useful first:

- Atlan, _Knowledge Graph vs RAG: When Each One Wins_ (June 2026) — the head-to-head task breakdown and the honest cost framing.
- Kloia, _Knowledge Base vs Knowledge Graph for LLM Systems_ (2026 guide) — best on the routing/hybrid architecture and the NeurIPS 2025 "KGs & Agentic Reasoning" direction.
- Salfati Group, _Graph RAG Guide 2025_ — the "factual constraint / hallucination reduction" argument and the Neo4j Manifesto lineage.
- _LLM-empowered Knowledge Graph Construction: A Survey_ (arXiv 2510.20345) — the construction-cost-collapse story, ontology-grounded extraction, competency-question workflows.
- _CypherBench_ (arXiv 2412.18702) — the sobering numbers on precise graph retrieval by model size; directly relevant to your Qwen-32B question.
- For your exact domain: _LegalGraphRAG_ (arXiv 2605.28120), _Capturing Legal Reasoning Paths from Facts to Law using Knowledge Graphs_ (K-CAP 2025), and _Knowledge Graph-Assisted LLM Post-Training for Enhanced Legal Reasoning_ (arXiv 2601.13806) — all model facts-and-rules structure rather than generic entities, which is closer to what CELEX cartel analysis needs than the usual entity-KG demos.
